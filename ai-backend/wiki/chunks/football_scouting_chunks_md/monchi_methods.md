“Ihave tried to educate myself, as Ibegan in this with zero, nothing,” he says. “Idid a Master’s in business management, went to London to study how English clubs worked. Idid lots of courses and Iread and watched things about communication, leadership; all the aspects and different ways of understanding things. I’ve read The Art Of War many times, about strategy, and shortening your path to success. But that is the only one book. There are lots.” Nine of the 13 masterclasses have been broadcast online so far — with Monchi speaking in Spanish over English subtitles. The first episode (titled The Luck Factor) got 35,000 views in its first month while altogether, they have already been watched almost 150,000 times.

“When we published it on Sevilla’s own YouTube channel, there were always doubts whether anyone would watch it,” he says. “Normally, that channel is just for Sevilla fans. But I’ve been very surprised and received many messages from people who I’d never have thought would watch. It has been generally positive — from fellow sporting directors in Spain and outside. I’ve plenty of other things Icould be doing but knowing people are watching motivates you to keep going.” During the first few classes, which went out when Spain was deep in lockdown, Monchi looked a little awkward standing in front of a TV screen showing a PowerPoint presentation managed from an iPad in his hand. He has since moved to a more comfortable spot on his couch and as the episodes have progressed, the production values have improved. It is clear he loves his job and loves telling people about it.

“I am enjoying it and Ican see how Ihave changed from the first episode to the most recent,” he says. “I am more open and able to communicate what Iwant. Iwatch a lot of videos from other leading figures who know how to communicate and transmit ideas, and try to learn from them. The best thing Ican say is that the time passes really quickly. That is because Ilike doing it.” The classes have the feel of an economics university lecture or workplace training seminar, with repetition of the most important phrases and recaps at the end of each lesson in an attempt to implant them in the mind of the viewer. The visuals are mostly bar charts and graphs that he methodically explains, not famous goal compilations or montages of players lifting trophies. When we see the office space at Sevilla’s training ground where he and his team work, it could pass for the regional headquarters of an IT services company.

Ahuge amount of information and detail is shared, which is surely of interest to scouts or spies from other clubs but also to ordinary fans wondering how the game’s elite go about identifying and signing players. Every single element of the squad planning process is broken down into its different stages and elements, with the “roadmap” having been developed and honed over Monchi’s career.

- There are “five stages to go through before making a signing”: watching players, selecting the targets, establishing the profile with the coach, negotiation and adaptation.
- When choosing a player, there are six factors to always take into account: physical profile, tactical/technical profile, psychological profile, financial conditions, adaptation time and future increase in value.
- Even watching potential targets is broken down into two tracks: “gross following’” from July to December of over 3,000 players in leagues all around the world and “net following” from January to May on a smaller group of just 500-550.

The amount of detail can be quite dry but Monchi does have a nice turn of phrase, especially as he grows more comfortably into his role as masterclass guru — “Our database is our treasure, the philosopher’s stone of the success of any planning,” he says in Masterclass Three.

Colour also comes through anecdotes from his career, whether being surprised by Sergio Ramos moving to Real Madrid late in the summer 2005 transfer window or being the wrong side of a transfer negotiation with Tottenham a few years later. Ramos had been pursued by Madrid all summer, said he was going to stay with his hometown club but then changed his mind just before the deadline. Monchi had no list of defensive replacements lined up, so had to take a punt on Ivica Dragutinovic of Belgium’s Standard Liege, who turned out to be good but still made Monchi take extra care to always have a list of targets for all positions.

Afamiliar anecdote from Spanish football pops up in episode six — The Theory Of The Lamp And The Table. This is a reworking of Rafa Benitez’s famous 2004 “Iwas hoping for a sofa and they bought me a lamp” complaint about those in charge of transfers during his time at Valencia when he wanted the board to sign a centre-forward in Samuel Eto’o but was instead presented with winger Fabian Canobbio. Of course, Monchi knew that particular lamp, having been following young prospect Canobbio in his days at Uruguayan club Penarol.

These communication devices are not a conscious effort to help us non-experts understand the complexities of his job, Monchi says, but a window into how his mind works.

“That is what I do every day,” he says. “What Itell is exactly what we do. When Italk about the roadmap and the planning, that is my real way of working. Idid nothing artificial to create the masterclasses.” Masterclass Seven includes a handy explainer of terms including “amortisation” and “net book value, which fans, and even some journalists, sometimes throw around without knowing their exact meanings. Monchi says he is not a good negotiator, claiming he lacks the required cool head. Instead, he puts his success in this area down to never attempting to humiliate or coerce the other side in transfer talks.

“Both parties have to be happy with a transfer or you are storing up problems for the future,” he says. “Tomorrow, Imight need to deal with them again.” Masterclass Eight shows Monchi’s interest in managing the “person” — making sure new players settle quickly into both the city and the club. The penchant for details even comes down to simple things such as warning new signings not to buy a car with green paint as that is the colour of Sevilla’s city rivals Real Betis. Always, the aim is to remove any possible excuse a player might have for not performing to their maximum level for their new team.

“It is not just about signing players but also, afterwards, accompanying and improving that player,” he says. “Not just dealing with the footballer, but with the person, that is so important.” Many times over the two hours-plus so far recorded, Monchi emphasises the importance of sticking closely to his process and that small details are all important, something he says he learned from Carlos Bilardo when being coached by Argentina’s 1986 World Cup-winning manager at Sevilla in the early 1990s.

“The smallest details contain the biggest successes. This is one of my mottos, the core of my philosophy,” Monchi says. “Because in the end, most of us sporting directors work practically the same way. We have Wyscout, InStat, a network of scouts. We travel, we watch football. The difference comes from the small details.” Monchi also gives plenty of credit to those who have worked with and under him along the way. Some of these men now have similar big jobs at other clubs, including Victor Orta at Leeds United, Real Valladolid’s Miguel Angel Gomez and Antonio Fernandez and Oscar Arias at current Segunda Division leaders Cadiz. Among his team now are former Sevilla and Spain left-back Fernando Navarro, Emilio de Dios and Ramon Vazquez.

“My travelling companions in this have always been fundamental,” Monchi says. “If Idid not have faith in my people, it would be impossible. Icannot follow 3,000 players for a whole season, so Ihave to believe that they (his staff) are doing good work, taking the correct decisions. Where Ienter is the final stage but before that, they are all fundamental. You have to surround yourself with strong people and good people.” Like most who reach the top in football, or in any other business, Monchi is not short of ego.

He says in Masterclass One that he favours a “mixed” model of transfer decision-making, where neither the president nor the manager has too much power in buying or selling players. When talking about scouting players, the manager only enters very deep into the process and while it is a positive if he likes the targets identified, he never gets the final say. The president and the board are there to provide the money but not pick the targets, or even get too involved in the negotiations with other clubs or agents.

As the episodes continue, you get the idea the sporting director is the most important figure at any club. And surely Monchi must know himself that, despite the mixed results of his two seasons at Roma from 2017 to 2019, he is still known as *the* iconic sporting director in Spanish football. Most clubs around Europe still want to either hire him or find someone as close as possible.

“Well,” he laughs, “Idon’t know about that. Ijust take it all as normal. There are other much more capable sporting directors in the world, with better careers. Ihave had the good fortune to be in a club where Ihave learned a lot. But you never should believe you are better than anybody, just in growing and improving each day. Ilearned a lot from Luis Aragones, who was my coach for two years at Sevilla. Luis always said not to stick your chest out as someone will break it. You have to treat the triumphs and successes the same.” Among the worst seasons in Sevilla’s last two decades was 2011-12, when Marcelino was sacked as coach in the February with the team out of both the Copa del Rey and Europa League, and struggling in La Liga. Monchi blames himself in Masterclass Six for what went wrong, absolving Marcelino of responsibility. Looking back at his successes and failures over the last few weeks as he recorded these videos has made him rethink certain things within his overall process.

“Life is a constant learning experience, and being a sporting director is too,” he says. “I’ve definitely learned some things by looking inside (while doing this). I’m remembering things that Ihad put aside in a drawer but want to include again now. Maybe, from all these masterclasses, the thing Ihave realised is that Ineed to be very close to the group, to the players. That is something especially important in this moment of pandemic, when we are so detached from each other. So Ihave been attending all the training sessions now that we have returned. Iwant to be even closer to the group than before.” This global epidemic and the lockdown has had a significant impact on Monchi’s process of identifying and recruiting talent for the next transfer window, and also helping the current squad perform at their best in a “new reality”, which is likely to last well into the 2020-21 season. That is something he is still thinking about at the moment and plans to fit into his final masterclass episodes.

“The next one will be on leadership, then big data and artificial intelligence,” he says. “The second-last episode will be on the sporting director during a pandemic; how to take that challenge on.” Finally, *The Athletic* wants to know if he can still just kick back and enjoy simply watching a game of football, whether it is a Champions League knockout-phase thriller or a mid-table Ligue 2 battle.

“Ilike to watch football, a lot, as my job and as my hobby,” Monchi says. “Both things. Isay it in the first episode. The only route to success is through hard work. I do not understand any other way. Ihave missed out on a lot with my wife, my children, my friends, my family, taken a lot of time from them, in order to do my job. That is the only way to be able to succeed in this. Or at least, I’ve not found another way.” **Monchi’s Masterclasses is a series of 13 videos available via Sevilla’s club YouTube channel**

**Title:** Monchi: Sevilla sporting director on his methods and mission

**Ramon Rodriguez Verdejo has been in football for more than 30 years, but there is every chance you have never heard that name. You might, however, know him better by a different one: Monchi.** He was never considered a star on the pitch but, now aged 52 and having swapped his kit for a suit two decades ago, he is arguably the most recognisable and revered figure at Sevilla, the hometown club where he has spent almost all his career.

You could even reasonably suggest that his success, and the methods behind them, have transformed the job he has performed for 20 years - that of sporting director.

As a player Monchi - an alternative Spanish name for someone called Ramon - spent 11 years at Sevilla, mostly as a back-up goalkeeper.

He played 126 games for the club - the only one he represented - until his retirement in 1999. The following year, he was appointed to the role he has since made his own.

At the time, with no experience and with Sevilla in the Spanish second division, Monchi studied other clubs' methods in order to seek inspiration for his own. FC Porto and Lyon were among those he looked at - clubs who won titles, sold their stars and started over again.

The main lesson was to accept losing your best players, but make sure you replace them with someone of similar quality for a good deal less money.

Sevilla didn't spend a penny on transfers during Monchi's first two years yet still managed to build a team that returned to La Liga under current Armenia head coach Joaquin Caparros.

It wasn't until the following season, in 2002, that Monchi was given the green light to make his first investment: 500,000 euros on full-back Dani Alves, who first arrived on loan from Brazilian club Bahia.

Six years later, Barcelona would pay about £23.5m to sign a player who was arguably the best in his position for a decade.

The Monchi revolution had begun.

Sevilla have enjoyed 20 consecutive seasons in La Liga and, on Monchi's watch, 'sell to grow' has firmly become their philosophy. Star players haven't tended to stay long - but usually long enough to lift a trophy.

Their recent trophy haul is impressive: Copa del Rey winners in 2007 and 2010; Uefa Cup winners in 2006 and 2007; Europa League winners in 2014, 2015, 2016 and 2020. Monchi had a major hand in it all, despite briefly leaving for a two-year spell with Roma between 2017-2019.

By the time he left - seeking a different challenge - he had helped the club earn around £180m in profit from transfer dealings.

He is back at the club that he loves now, where he is widely respected and where on a typically bright Andalusian day thoughts are turning to Wednesday's Champions League meeting with Chelsea - with the winner of the game likely to top their group.

After a year unlike any other, both clubs can take comfort from their already guaranteed progress to the knockout stages.

"The truth is it has been quite an intense time, facing unknown scenarios and managing issues that go beyond a sporting director's responsibilities," Monchi tells BBC Sport.

"Negotiations have been a little bit more complicated. We had to rely on the work we had already done before lockdown. We assigned new tasks to our scouts, who reviewed again and again outstanding players we had been following for a while.

"We even planned different signings depending on future scenarios, including the cancellation of La Liga." The signing of Ivan Rakitic is perhaps the standout of several typically shrewd moves executed by Monchi and his team in the south of Spain this summer.

The Croatian midfielder was signed for about 2.5m euros in from Schalke in 2011 and left in 2014 for Barcelona in a deal worth about 20m euros. In September, Sevilla agreed a nominal 1.5m euro fee to take him back, now aged 32.

While those deals are standard practice for Monchi, things have changed for him in 2020 - or rather, the Covid-19 crisis has accelerated change that was already under way. It has led to a major boost at the club's new data-driven research and development department, which was created in May 2019.

The scouting process in football is evolving quickly, and new forms of data are becoming increasingly valued. New stats and metrics such as expected goals (xG) are taken into account when making transfer decisions.

An increasing number of elite football clubs are trying to get the most out of the numbers, as has been done in baseball and basketball for years. Many of those employed tend to have no football background, but it doesn't matter as long as there are results.

"We compare the subjective point of view of our scouts with data's objective criteria," Monchi says.

"The fact they both almost always agree encourages us to make decisions. We trusted big data more this time because we have a more developed big data department.

"Although our platform is not 100% complete, it has helped us much more when analysing interesting players. Our data was not as accurate a year ago as it is today.

"We had 'small data' years ago. Ascouting report on Microsoft Word was data. If a player is right or left footed, that's data too...

"Obviously, now we have better processed data, more information and we are more willing to rely on it. Confidence comes with use. You don't use something you don't trust.

"Nonetheless, we can't disregard the subjective point of view of a scout. What we have to do is to join both approaches." Monchi and his team plan to expand this data revolution to also help inform various other sections of the club's operations, such as ticketing and marketing.

But building a system that can find the right players at the right price remains the priority. The algorithm they have developed analyses over 18,000 players that could fit the team. And behind the scenes, a new kind of transfer target is in Monchi's sights.

"We are currently talking with an artificial intelligence and machine learning expert with no football experience to join us. We are fighting with his current company, so to speak," he says.

"We want to keep growing. If you decide to build a research and development department, you have to provide people who would add value by understanding data from different angles." Because of Monchi's success at Sevilla, an increasing number of football clubs have tried to follow their model. Clubs such as Benfica, Sporting Lisbon, Monaco and Ajax have flourished... and make even more profit than the Andalusians.

Others have preferred to rely on Monchi disciples, such as Victor Orta, who spent seven years under his tutelage and now fits in well with Marcelo Bielsa's Leeds United.

But big transfers remain the strategy favoured by many others.

That's the case of Chelsea, who drew 0-0 with Sevilla in the club's earlier Champions League meeting at Stamford Bridge this season. Kai Havertz, Hakim Ziyech, Timo Werner, Ben Chilwell and Edouard Mendy - signed for a total of about £222m this summer - could all feature at the Ramon Sanchez Pizjuan on Wednesday evening.

"Iwould like to believe the Chelsea directors are perfectly aware of their finances and their earning capacity, I'm convinced they are," Monchi says. "If you make an investment it's because your budget allows it.

"Chelsea spent the last few years kind of hidden in the football market [in part perhaps because of their transfer ban], but they have done some very good deals. Young players with a great projection that will prove profitable, on and off the pitch." Monchi has never hidden his fascination with English football. In fact, he spent six months in London in 2013 to improve his English and do some research.

"Iwas looking for business information," he says. "Premier League clubs have a really advanced business model that Iwanted to implement at Sevilla.

"They are able to make football really profitable through television, marketing and a sense of belonging - everything that made English football double in income to Spanish at that time." Monchi sought inspiration in England at a time when English clubs sought their own Monchi. Today, many Premier League sides - such as Manchester City, Liverpool, Leeds United and Arsenal - do indeed have a sporting director. Manchester United are one club which does not.

"There are lots of successful clubs that entrust recruitment policies to different people: the general manager, the head coach or the sporting director. Iprefer the last option, obviously, because it is the most balanced," Monchi says.

"It doesn't mean that Manchester United don't have a scouting department. Of course they do.

"They don't have Txiki Begiristain [Manchester City] or Michael Edwards [Liverpool] - both old friends of mine - but I'm pretty sure there are qualified professionals to make those decisions at Old Trafford. We are talking about top clubs, Ihave little or even nothing to teach them." After 20 years making tough decisions, Monchi claims to know how to withstand the pressure. He is happy in his work and still feels he has plenty to offer. But he has already started to think about the timing of his own next transfer.

"Iowe my family, my friends and myself too," he says. "Ineed to give that time back to them. Including me. That's why Iwon't be here for long.

"Everyone else comes second. I've known my wife since we were 15. My children, aged 27 and 21, were raised in these circumstances. Iprioritise the club so much that Isacrifice my well-being. But Iconsider myself privileged. I've been doing what Idreamed of when Iwas a kid.

"Six hours of sleep is enough for me. Iget up early to exercise, but during this time I'm also working since my smartphone and my computer are on. Disconnection doesn't make sense in my position.

"This will last as long as Ifeel happy. Ihaven't marked a date on the calendar. Today Icome to work with the same ambition. Iwill walk away when Ifeel anxious when Iopen my office door.

"I'm lucky to have friends who don't get mad when Ipromise to call back... and I do it five days later."
